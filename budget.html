Sub-objective

What this component sets out to do

To design and implement a budget–experience decision intelligence component that produces an optimized budget allocation strategy indicating how a tourist’s financial resources should be distributed across accommodation, activities and transportation to maximize experiential value — without generating day-wise itineraries or schedules.

Sub-objective, Topic Assessment Form

Tasks

  1. Analyse existing tourism budgeting and cost-handling approaches.
  2. Identify experience-impact factors that influence satisfaction independently of scheduling.
  3. Define a quantitative experience value representation relating spending levels to perceived experience quality.
  4. Develop an AI-assisted decision model for optimal budget distribution.
  5. Generate an allocation output consumable by the itinerary and routing components.
  6. Evaluate how allocation strategies affect experience quality under varying budget constraints.
What it excludes

An allocation profile, not a plan

This component does not generate day-wise itineraries. It does not produce schedules.

Its output is a strategic allocation profile: how the money should be divided across categories.

Novelty

The novelty claim

A budget–experience decision intelligence mechanism outputting a strategic financial allocation profile, focused on maximizing experiential value rather than validating or adjusting itineraries. Unlike itinerary-based budgeting, it operates independently of day-wise scheduling and provides cost–experience trade-off intelligence as a standalone decision-support output.

Method

Four objectives

The method is organized as four objectives.

  1. Existing approachesFive approach families; limitations reproduced on the project’s own data.
  2. Sri Lankan cost dataA localized pricing reference and a 117-feature training table.
  3. Spending against valueResponse curves per category and budget level.

Objective 1

Existing approaches

reports/existing_approaches_review.md reviews five approach families, with a comparison matrix and three limitations, L1–L3.

reports/existing_approaches_evidence.md then reproduces each limitation on the project’s own data, rather than asserting it from literature.

Objective 2

Sri Lankan cost data and feature table v2

A localized pricing reference

  • Entry fees by district
  • Transport LKR/km by mode
  • Room-night rates
  • Destination index
  • Registered supply

117 features in training_table_v2.csv, on which the deployed models train: v1 plus traveller profile and local pricing.

Engineering decision

Train/serve parity

The service computes the same features per request through the same code that built the training table (scripts/feature_engineering.py).

It refuses to start without config/pricing_reference.json.

Train/serve feature parity is enforced by tests.

Missing inputs

Inferred, and said so

Requests may omit interests, visitor_market or primary_destination_locality_id.

Anything omitted is inferred where possible, and listed back in the response’s profile_inferred field.

Objective 3

Spending against experiential value

How to read these effects

The data are observational and synthetic. Every effect is how predicted experience differs between trips that planned different spending, with the other recorded factors at each trip’s own values. It is an association, not a controlled experiment.

0.0493 test R² with every spending feature removed, against 0.0538 with them.

Most of what the model knows about experience comes from who travels and how (interest, transport mode, pace), not from how much is spent. Removing every spending feature moves test R² only from 0.0538 to 0.0493.

Objective 4

The optimized split

/budget/allocate chooses the split across accommodation, transport, attraction, activity and food_other by maximizing:

mean ensemble experience
  − lambda × ensemble std
  − mu     × P(over budget)
  − kappa  × shift from the typical split

Implemented scope. The TAF sub-objective names three categories (accommodation, activities, transportation). The implemented optimizer splits five, adding attraction and food_other.

Constrained within

  1. Local-price floors: room-nights and distance at Sri Lankan rates.
  2. The 5th–95th percentile of spend observed for that budget level.
  3. Any category_bounds the tourist sets. Equal min and max locks a category.

The response reports

  • split_strategy_used
  • predicted_experience_gain over the typical split
  • split_notes

The ceiling row is deliberately invalid as a model: it includes outcome columns, such as actual costs. It is run to find how much of the experience score any recorded variable can explain.

The finding that decides the claim

The forecast is weak, so the contribution is the allocation

Experience prediction is weak: R² is 0.0538. The ceiling test explains why. Experience score is nearly independent of every recorded variable, not merely the planning-time ones. R² only reaches 0.0715 even with actual costs included.

Therefore the component’s contribution is the allocation mechanism, not the forecast. This page does not claim that the forecast works.

That is why the comparison below is the result that matters. Both arms of the optimizer comparison use the same predicted values, so it measures allocation rather than prediction.

Part 2: the optimizer against the tourist’s own plan

400 test-split trips. Both arms use the same candidate pools and the same predicted values.

“Value” here means predicted value, from a model with R² 0.0538. The optimizer’s ensemble is fitted on one half of the data, and its allocations are scored by a model fitted on the other half, so the comparison is not self-scored.

57.8% of trips where the optimizer delivered more value, working with a mean attraction budget of LKR 25,652 against the plan’s LKR 49,451.

The optimizer works with roughly half the plan’s attraction money and still delivers more value in 57.8% of trips. The gain comes from selecting by value per rupee, not from a larger budget. It selects more attractions with less money: 18.01 on average, against the plan’s 15.46.

Caveat: only attraction value is scored

An arm that gives attractions a larger budget therefore wins by construction, and money taken from accommodation or food is not counted against it. The attraction-budget rows are shown so that this effect can be separated.

Here the caveat runs the other way. The optimizer works with LKR 25,652 for attractions against the plan’s LKR 49,451, and still delivers more value in 57.8% of trips. In this comparison the plan is the arm with the larger attraction budget, so the construction favours the plan, and the 57.8% is conservative.

What the caveat genuinely limits is any claim about the whole-trip split, since money in the other categories is never scored. The comparison therefore measures how well each arm selects attractions. It does not show that the optimizer’s split across the other categories is better for the trip as a whole.

Stated limitations

Stated limitations

Seven stated limitations

  1. Experience is hard to predict on this data. This is reported rather than worked around.
  2. Accommodation bands are weak evidence. They come from undated secondary listings with wide spreads, and are usable for plausibility checking only.
  3. Candidate pools are synthesized. The dataset records trip totals, not per-attraction selections.
  4. accessibility_requirement is 89% null.
  5. 3 of 78 cost anchors are unmapped, affecting 342 of 12,000 trips.
  6. Attractions are treated as independent. Two sites in one locality share travel cost in reality; the formulation does not model that.
  7. Synthetic data throughout, so external validity is not established.
