Sub-objective

What this component sets out to do

To develop an AI-based preference modeling and recommendation module that learns tourist interests and behaviours to provide personalized attraction recommendations aligned with user preferences and travel context.

Sub-objective, Topic Assessment Form

Tasks

  1. Analyse preference data and recommendation techniques.
  2. Identify relevant features: interests, travel style and past selections.
  3. Design an AI model to learn and represent preferences.
  4. Generate personalized recommendations.
  5. Integrate with the itinerary planning system.
  6. Evaluate accuracy and relevance.
Novelty

The novelty claim

A dynamic, preference-weighted recommendation engine that continuously learns and prioritizes tourist interests in real time to generate adaptive destination rankings. Unlike static or rule-based recommendation systems, it updates preference weights from user interactions throughout the trip.

Novelty, Topic Assessment Form

The mechanism is built and runs end-to-end. Whether adapting during a trip improves rankings could not be shown on this dataset; see stated limitations.

Founding finding

Stated interests are weakly predictive. Behaviour is learnable.

What tourists do

11.2 point lift. A tourist selects a category they have already chosen 50.9% of the time, against 39.7% for a category new to them.

Without that gap there would be nothing for a preference model to learn. The rest of this component is built on it.

Method, part 1

The preference learner

Per tourist and per category, a Beta posterior over “would they choose an attraction of this kind”.

alpha_c, beta_c   pseudo-counts, seeded from the stated interests
taste_c           = alpha_c / (alpha_c + beta_c)
confidence_c      = n_c / (n_c + kappa)

Seeding the prior from stated interests is what makes a cold tourist something other than an average tourist.

Three mechanisms on top

Mechanisms layered on the Beta posterior. Source: models/preference_learner.py.
MechanismWhat it does
Graded rewardsAn accept, a rating, a click that went nowhere and a silent skip are different strengths of evidence.
Category couplingAn observation on Beach also moves Scenic, damped by a similarity matrix estimated from co-selection, not asserted.
RecencyPseudo-counts decay toward the prior with a half-life, so a taste that shifts mid-trip is tracked rather than averaged away.

Two of these mechanisms, graded rewards and category coupling, score higher on pooled AUC when removed. See the ablation.

Trip-level tolerances

The learner also learns how far this tourist will travel and what they will pay. A rejection for a trip-level reason, such as “too far”, updates those tolerances. It is deliberately not counted against the category.

Method, part 2

The ranker

A gradient-boosted model over three blocks of tourist features (trip context, stated preferences and the learned profile), scored against the candidate’s own attributes, and blended with a linear scorer by profile maturity.

Source: models/ranker.py

Why the blend is not a hedge

The learned ranker beats the incumbent (the Phase 3c linear scorer, re-scored with derived factors) on tourists with history, and loses on cold-start ones. So profile_maturity decides the regime per tourist, rather than one model being chosen globally.

Cold start is the normal case in this data, not an edge case: the component’s report puts 92.5% of the user base in the cold band.

The NDCG@5 results show the split: the linear scorer is best on cold start, the personalized model is best where there is history.

Diversification

Output is diversified with MMR over categories.

A day of five beaches is a worse answer than four beaches and a viewpoint, even when it scores higher.

The honest reading

Stated preference contributes nothing once POI attributes and trip context are in the model. Its drop is negative (−0.0020, sd 0.0019): shuffling it slightly improves AUC.

That is the measurement motivating the component, not a result to hide. It agrees with the founding finding: what tourists declare carries little signal.

Aside

A serving constraint

time_fit and budget_fit are logged per interaction. They are not properties of an attraction. A model using them as logged could only score attractions a tourist had already seen, which rules out ranking the full catalogue.

They were recomputed from attributes plus trip context, with travel time taken from registry distance at 35 km/h. Agreement with the logged values: time_fit 71.3%, budget_fit 92.4%.

55 of the 63 features are servable.

Against the never-updated stated-interest prior, the learner gains +0.0078 AUC, 95% CI [0.0031, 0.0128]. The gain is small, but the interval excludes zero.

The report’s own defence is that the measurement that matters is the trend across evidence bands, not the pooled AUC: the full learner improves as evidence accumulates, and the rule it replaced gets worse.

Stated limitations

What this evaluation cannot show

Limitation

Adapting during the trip shows no measurable gain

The adaptive mechanism works end-to-end and is reachable from the running product. But under a clean per-session protocol, adapting during the held-out window barely moves either metric, and both confidence intervals span zero.

+0.0009 NDCG from adaptation. Confidence interval spans zero.

+0.0021 AUC from adaptation. Confidence interval spans zero.

Why an earlier, larger figure is not used

An earlier phase reported +0.0091 AUC for the same idea. That figure pooled a tourist’s held-out interactions into one ranking group, so items late in the group were scored by a profile that had already seen outcomes from the same group. It was not a measurement of adaptation.

What is real, and what is not shown

The repeat structure the engine exploits is real: 50.9% against 39.7%. What this dataset cannot show is that exploiting it moves a ranking metric over the one or two held-out sessions per tourist it contains.
